It also identifies an important distinction: **The companies that benefit most from building AI infrastructure will not necessarily be the companies that own and operate that infrastructure.**

## 2. Four important improvements to your original thesis

### A. AGI could accelerate infrastructure demand, but also make AI dramatically more efficient

Your recursive-improvement argument cuts both ways.

More capable AI could help design better chips, algorithms, and data centers, accelerating the development of even more capable systems.

That could substantially increase demand for intelligence.

But it could also reduce the computing resources required to accomplish a given task.

Consider a simplified example:

| Scenario | AI workload growth | Energy efficiency improvement | Total electricity demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Explosive adoption | 24× | 5× | 4.8× |
| Rapid efficiency gains | 24× | 10× | 2.4× |
| Efficiency breakthrough | 24× | 30× | 0.8× |

These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. They assume comparable workloads and hold other data-center electricity requirements constant.

**The possibility of AGI doesn't automatically guarantee that GPU or electricity demand will explode.**

Your thesis becomes stronger when it recognizes that total demand depends on both the number of tasks being performed and the resources required per task.

### B. The financial bottleneck matters as much as the physical bottleneck

This is the biggest lesson from the recent neocloud selloff.

A company can correctly predict enormous demand for its services and still be a terrible investment.

Consider a company that borrows $20 billion to construct AI infrastructure.

If the company earns excellent returns on that investment, shareholders benefit.

But if GPUs become obsolete faster than expected, rental prices fall, or financing becomes more expensive, shareholders could lose money despite excellent customer demand.

Reuters reported in July that the combined capital expenditures of Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle were growing faster than their operating cash flow, creating pressure on free cash flow. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/ai-investment-boom-puts-big-techs-free-cash-flow-under-pressure-2026-07-22/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That leads to a critical addition:

**Your investment thesis should prioritize companies that profit from infrastructure demand, not simply companies that spend the most building infrastructure.**

### C. Some infrastructure is more durable than other infrastructure

Consider the useful life of different assets.

A GPU might become economically obsolete within a few years as newer, more efficient chips arrive.

But a newly constructed substation or transmission line might remain useful for decades.

That difference matters enormously.

I'd distinguish between two types of infrastructure investments:

**Technology infrastructure:** Semiconductors, memory, servers, networking, and GPU clusters. These offer substantial growth but can experience rapid technological disruption.

**Physical infrastructure:** Electricity generation, power distribution, cooling systems, and grid equipment. These have longer replacement cycles and often support customers outside AI.

However, longer asset life doesn't automatically produce a better stock return. Construction costs, regulation, competition, and valuations still matter.

### D. AI companies will increasingly design their own hardware

This is a particularly important development from October 12.

Microsoft announced plans to potentially deploy more than one million custom Maia 300 AI chips in 2027.

Microsoft claims its system delivers approximately 40% more useful computing per megawatt than leading GPU systems, although that comparison hasn't been independently established. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/microsoft-plans-huge-deployment-its-own-ai-chips-with-an-eye-saving-costs-2026-10-12/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This illustrates another opportunity within your thesis.

AI companies may increasingly compete to build more efficient computing systems.

That benefits the advanced semiconductor manufacturing ecosystem, not necessarily every existing chip supplier equally.

**TSM and AVGO could benefit from AI hardware diversification, while NVIDIA could face more competition for some workloads.**

It also demonstrates why betting exclusively on one company is risky, even when the overall technology thesis is correct.

The third scenario deserves serious attention because technological success and financial success can diverge.

# 6. Your improved investment thesis — ready to use

Here is how I would rewrite your complete argument into a coherent, reusable statement.

### The AI/AGI Infrastructure Supercycle Thesis (2026–2035)

I believe we're approaching a historic economic transformation driven by increasingly capable artificial intelligence, potentially culminating in AGI between 2027 and 2029.

My central investment thesis is that the economic impact of AI won't come simply from developing an intelligent model. It will come from deploying vast quantities of machine intelligence throughout the global economy.

As AI systems become capable of performing increasingly complex professional tasks autonomously, businesses will have powerful economic incentives to replace or augment expensive human labor with scalable digital intelligence.

One successful AGI system would be only the beginning. The economic opportunity lies in deploying millions of specialized and general-purpose AI agents simultaneously across software development, research, engineering, medicine, finance, manufacturing, and other industries.

These agents will require substantial computing resources for inference, reasoning, planning, execution, verification, and continuous operation.

As the cost of intelligence declines, previously uneconomical applications will become viable. This could create a positive feedback loop in which cheaper intelligence unlocks additional demand, increasing total AI workloads despite substantial improvements in computing efficiency.

Furthermore, increasingly capable AI may accelerate AI research itself, helping develop better algorithms, models, chips, and infrastructure. This creates the possibility of accelerating technological progress.

However, digital intelligence depends on physical infrastructure.

Semiconductor manufacturing, advanced packaging, memory, high-speed networking, electrical generation, transmission systems, transformers, cooling equipment, and data-center construction cannot expand as quickly as software capabilities.

**I expect the mismatch between accelerating demand for intelligence and slower-growing physical infrastructure capacity to create persistent bottlenecks.**

Companies controlling critical, difficult-to-replicate parts of that supply chain could capture disproportionate economic value.

I believe the most attractive opportunities will often be found in companies providing essential infrastructure to multiple AI developers rather than companies making highly leveraged bets on operating individual data centers.

The investment opportunity also extends beyond the moment AGI is achieved. Even if AI development eventually plateaus, deploying existing capabilities across the economy could require years of additional computing infrastructure.

My investment horizon is approximately 2026–2035, with particular attention to the infrastructure expansion expected during 2027–2030.

I recognize that AI progress does not guarantee investment returns. Rapid efficiency improvements, falling computing prices, financing constraints, competition, regulation, and excessive valuations could cause individual infrastructure investments to underperform even while AI adoption accelerates.

**Therefore, my strategy is to invest in financially resilient companies with durable competitive advantages, strong demand, sustainable capital requirements, and reasonable valuations, while maintaining smaller positions in more speculative infrastructure operators.**

I will evaluate the thesis using observable indicators: AI capabilities and adoption, paid inference demand, aggregate computing and electricity consumption, infrastructure deployment, supplier profitability, financing conditions, and actual returns on invested capital.

**My fundamental belief is that intelligence is becoming an increasingly scalable economic resource, but the physical systems required to produce and distribute it will remain comparatively scarce.**

That scarcity creates one of the most consequential investment opportunities of the next decade.
